Checking the AI curation

How the AI curation compares with the manual curation on the publications of 2017–2026, everyday experiments and screens taken apart, and whether its short viral mappings hold up against their publications.

By Claude, for the Drakkar curation team · 18 November 2026 · from the live vh descriptions of Drakkar on that day, read-only, publications of 2017–2026

In short

  • Screens and everyday experiments are compared apart. In the runs the AI curated, the manual curation had left out the large screens and the AI took them: a comparison mixing them compares different literature.
  • On non-screens, the AI records more per publication: a median of 3 descriptions against 2, one per experiment, method and strain tested.
  • It records the construct the experiment used, not a region concluded from it: a human mapping in 40.9 % of its non-screens against 32.9 %, and longer ones.
  • It names the assay as performed and writes a note on every description; its lower identities come from UniProt 2026, which dropped most viral strain entries.
  • Per screen, the two curations differ little, except in the method name.
  • Short viral peptides: without the four publications holding more than 20 each, both sides record as many where the peptide itself was tested (6.2 per 100 publications each); the manual curation records more in cell-based experiments (6.5 against 1.7), where the AI's rules refuse a region concluded from deletions or mutants. Every one of the AI's short viral sequences was found in its publication.

How the comparison was made

The unit is the publication with at least one live vh description, published in 2017–2026. A publication is AI-curated when its last curation pass was the AI's, otherwise manual.

A screen is a publication where one screening method (affinity capture read by mass spectrometry, proximity labelling, a library or an array) gives at least 20 descriptions; a non-screen has fewer than 20 descriptions in all; the few publications in neither class (a large panel of a non-screen method, several small experiments) are left out. The two sets are compared apart: in a non-screen, every description is a curator's decision; a manual screen was imported by script from one table of the publication and a threshold.

Besides who curated, two things differ between the sides. UniProt: the manual curation chose its entries in UniProt 2021, the AI in UniProt 2026, which dropped most viral strain entries (¹ marks what it touches). The years: the AI curated the publications of 2023–2026, the manual non-screens are mostly of 2017–2022. Every difference below was checked on 2023–2026 alone, and holds there unless it says otherwise.

Non-screens: four differences

AI curationManual curation

1. The AI records more per publication

A median of 3 descriptions per non-screen against 2, and 2 human–viral pairs against 1. Not more methods per pair (1.41 against 1.44 on average): more pairs. Why: its rules ask for one description per experiment, method and strain tested, supplementary figures included.

2. The AI records the construct, not the region concluded

The AI has a human mapping in 40.9 % of its non-screens against 32.9 %, a viral mapping as often, and longer mappings on both sides (Figure 3). Why: a mapping, by its rules, is the sequence the experiment used, never a region concluded from the results; when deletions show that a region matters, it records the fragment the experiment tested, which holds that region.

3. The AI names the assay as performed

On the non-screens of 2023–2026, the generic term coimmunoprecipitation makes 13.0 % of the manual descriptions per publication, 3.2 % of the AI's, which uses the precise anti-tag or anti-bait co-IP. Why: its rules take the closest PSI-MI term to the assay performed; a capture read by mass spectrometry is named as the capture, the read-out written in its note.

4. UniProt, and the notes

13.0 % of the AI's viral mappings are below 100 % identity against 0.7 % of the manual ones, and it uses mature proteins more. Why: UniProt 2026 no longer has most strain entries; the AI records the publication's strain on the closest entry, its residues kept, as its rules ask. And every AI publication and description carries a note saying why each choice was made; on the manual side, 7 % of the non-screens have a note on every description.

The two curations did not choose their entries in the same UniProt. The manual curation worked on UniProt 2021_02, whose viral section held 4.91 million entries: 4,893,260 unreviewed (TrEMBL) and 17,014 reviewed (Swiss-Prot), many of them single strains and isolates. In June 2025, UniProt announced that UniProtKB would keep only the sequences of reference proteomes, besides Swiss-Prot and entries with important extra data, its viral reference proteomes following the exemplar genomes of the ICTV; the restriction came with release 2026_02. Release 2026_03, which the AI curation worked on, holds 1.11 million viral entries: 1,092,202 unreviewed and 17,529 reviewed. A strain that had its own entry when the manual curation chose it now often has none, and the rules then assign the closest entry by sequence.

Screens: little difference per screen

Per screen, the two curations record as many methods and as often a mapping; the AI's screens are larger at threshold 20 (median 232 descriptions against 112), not at threshold 10 nor on 2023–2026 alone. The visible difference is the method name: the same mass-spectrometry screen is mass spectrometry studies of complexes by hand (45.9 % of the manual screens) and an anti-tag or anti-bait co-IP for the AI (point 3). What a screen comparison must answer is which tables and cutoffs each side took: that needs the publications themselves, and is left to the blind benchmark.

Focus: short viral peptides

What is counted. A short viral peptide is a viral mapping of 5 to 20 residues, counted as a unique sequence: once per publication, however many descriptions repeat it (a peptide used as bait against 900 human proteins is one peptide). An outlier is a publication holding more than 20 unique short viral peptides by itself, a phage-display screen or a peptide array: there are 4 in 2017–2026, the next publication holding 18.

Four publications hold a large part of all the short viral peptides: 34799561, 37100772, 38473724 on the manual side (560 peptides between them), 38698856 on the AI side (37). Which side curated them is chance, so the comparison is made without them (Table 2 gives every set, with and without).

Without the outliers, the two curations extract as many short viral peptides wherever the peptide itself was tested (synthetic or purified, titrated by ITC or fluorescence polarization, arrayed, crystallised, measured by SPR or NMR): 6.2 per 100 publications for the AI, 6.2 for the manual curation. The difference is all in cell-based experiments (co-IP, pull-down, BRET, two-hybrid): 6.5 per 100 by hand against 1.7 for the AI. Over all experiments, 12.3 against 7.8.

AI curationManual curation

Why. In a cell-based experiment, a protein is expressed or captured as a construct, rarely 5–20 residues long. A short viral sequence recorded there is either a peptide fused to a tag, or a region concluded from deletions or mutants that lose binding. The two curations' rules differ exactly there. The manual curation collected sequences of interaction domains “clearly identified in the article” (Vinland, PNAS 2024, SI Appendix, criterion 10), which includes a region its authors identify by deletion mapping. The AI's rules (Drakkar's glossary and curation rules) keep only a subsequence “the experiment shows is enough for the interaction”, “never a region concluded from the results”: the stricter reading of the necessary and sufficient interfaces the PNAS paper looks for. Both rules exclude mutated proteins. The AI records the fragment the experiment tested instead, which holds the region (difference 2); its notes say so, 146 of its 563 non-screens declining a region as a mapping, for example: “Residues 139-154 are a region concluded from the mutants, so not a mapping.” (42253058). Whether each manual region concluded from deletions is right, or each AI fragment, is a question for a biologist reading the publication, not for counts.

Every short viral mapping of the AI curation, checked

The counts above say how many short viral peptides each side records, not whether they are right. For the AI side, that was checked one by one, on the live descriptions of 16 November 2026: each of the 99 distinct short viral sequences the AI curation had then recorded, in any publication year, was looked for in its publication's full text and supplementary files, as downloaded for the curation. All 99 are there: 62 written in the publication's files, 32 placed exactly where the residue range of their note puts them on the protein, 5 printed only in a figure, read by eye. None is ambiguously placed on its protein; one is at 94 % identity, a strain difference its note states. 92 of the 99 were new to Drakkar. The manual side could not be checked the same way: its full texts are not on disk.

What it does not say

It never says which side is right where the two curations differ: a difference is a question, not an error. That is for a biologist judging both sides against the publication without knowing which is the AI's.

How the numbers were made: shares compared by Fisher's exact test, per-publication numbers by Mann–Whitney, intervals Wilson 95 %. Checked at screen thresholds 10, 50 and 100 and on the publications of 2023–2026 alone. Every number of this page recomputed by an independent path, SQL on Drakkar itself: all identical.
